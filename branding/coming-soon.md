## Voice

Calm, precise, slightly literary. Short sentences.

Speak to people who already know they forget what they saved. Confidence without hype. No exclamation marks.

Include professions by situation (“papers, threads, docs, lectures”), not a laundry list of titles.

“You” is the reader. Never say “users.”

**Anti-copy:** productivity-hustle, “unlock your potential,” second-brain jargon without a concrete loop, waitlists, pricing, MCP, WhatsApp, or a feature grid. Coming soon should feel like a quiet room, not a launch.

## Positioning

Nook is for curious high-volume consumers — founders, developers, researchers, doctors, and similar — who read, watch, and save more than they can recall.

Shared behavior, not job titles: they capture opportunistically and fail at retrieval. Bookmark and notes apps already lost them because organizing was the price of getting anything back.

**Promise:** capture anywhere, ask anything, get answers with sources.

**Mental model:** throw it in Nook, ask later. No folders, tags, or collections in the story.

**Name:** Nook (product). A **nook** is one saved item. **Nook this** / **nooked** is the capture verb. Use the verb on the page; it is the brand habit.

## Research notes (internal)

Source: synthesis of cited workplace-search studies (McKinsey, IDC, Atlassian, Glean, APQC, Starmind, Betterstacks, Gartner, plus academic re-finding work). Use it to name the *cost*, not to turn the coming-soon page into a whitepaper.

**On-page claim (honest, defensible):** knowledge workers spend about a fifth of the day looking for information, much of it something they already saw.

## What Nook can take off a day (estimate, not a product study)

Nobody has measured Nook. Cognitive load is not the same as hours spent searching. State this plainly. Give a **bounded analog**, not a promise.

### What Nook can and cannot touch

Nook only helps if the person actually captured the thing, and only for **their** library.

**In scope:** re-finding a paper, highlight, thread, video, screenshot, or note they nooked; asking in plain language instead of hunting tabs, Downloads, bookmarks, and the wrong Cmd+F.

**Out of scope (most of the famous 1.8 hours):** email (McKinsey: ~28% of the week), tracking down colleagues, meetings, company Slack/Notion that was never nooked, first-time discovery of unknown information.

Nook cannot “give back a fifth of the day.” That fifth is mixed enterprise search. Nook is a slice: personal re-finding plus the residue those hunts leave.

### Clock time (defensible range)

Best analog for *improved retrieval of existing knowledge*: McKinsey ([The Social Economy, 2012](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-social-economy)) estimated a searchable record of knowledge could cut **information-search time by as much as 35%**, returning **~6% of the workweek** (~25–30 minutes in an 8-hour day). That was enterprise social, not a personal second brain — treat it as an upper analog for retrieval, not a Nook KPI.

Apply 35% only to the **personal re-finding** slice, not to 1.8 hours:

- Conservative slice: APQC looking + recreating ≈ **~1 hour/day**; 35% ≈ **~20 minutes/day**
- Upper vendor slice: Betterstacks “already found” ≈ **~1.4 hours/day**; 35% ≈ **~30 minutes/day**; a habitual capturer might do better, but we do not use their 62% product claim

**Working range for a heavy user who nooks as they go: about 20–45 minutes of hunt/rework time returned per day.** Below 20 if they capture little. Not 1.5–2 hours.

### Cognitive load (the larger, unmeasured part)

Load is working-memory occupancy, not minutes. Three mechanisms, all real, none quantified for Nook:

1. **Cognitive offloading** — storing “I might need this later” outside the head frees working memory for the current task (Risko & Gilbert and related offloading work). Capture has to be cheaper than remembering, which is why Nook’s silent one-action capture matters. Offloading only reduces load if retrieval is trusted; a graveyard of bookmarks does not.
2. **Fewer hunts = fewer switches** — a “where did I see that?” trip is often 5–6 places. Each switch leaves **attention residue** (Leroy, 2009): part of attention stays on the unfinished hunt, so the original work runs at reduced capacity.
3. **Closure** — an unanswered hunt stays open. Asking Nook and getting a cited answer (or a clean miss) is closer to finishing the search than leaving 12 tabs open.

Do not convert residue into hours. Do not say “five hunts × 23 minutes = two lost hours.”

### What we will and will not say

**Internal / later product:** habitual users may get back on the order of **20–45 minutes a day** of hunting, plus less residue from those hunts. That is a literature analog, not a measured Nook result.

**Coming-soon page:** keep the cost line (“a fifth of the day looking… much of it already seen”). Do **not** print a savings number. The page sells the loop (nook it, ask later), not a fake ROI. Quiet text link: **Why this exists** → `/why`.

**`/why` page:** the argument, with citations. Still no Nook ROI number. Name the analog (searchable knowledge can cut *search* time; McKinsey’s 35% is enterprise, not us) and stop there.

**If a savings line is needed later (not this pass):** “Built for the part of the day you spend looking for something you already saw” — mechanism, not a number.
